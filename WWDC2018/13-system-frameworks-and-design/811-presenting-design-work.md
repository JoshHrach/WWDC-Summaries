# Presenting Design Work
**WWDC18 · Session 811** · [Watch](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2018/811/)

_Platforms:_ General (design practice, all platforms)

## Overview
A concise, high-energy talk from an Apple designer sharing ten hard-won lessons about presenting design work effectively. The session addresses the challenge that good ideas frequently fail not because of weak design but because of a weak presentation — and that the skills required to communicate design well are distinct from the skills required to create it.

The talk is organized around three parties in any presentation: the presenter, the audience, and the central idea. It draws on storytelling research (referencing Nancy Duarte's presentation framework) and communicates through an intentionally absurd running example of a smart toaster app to demonstrate narrative structure, empathy building, and clarity of problem statement.

## Key Topics

### Clarifying Objectives
- Before presenting, define what success looks like and work backwards from there
- Identify the specific questions you need the audience to help answer
- State goals and agenda upfront; address the audience's objectives, not just your own
- Establish the big picture before diving into details

### Embracing Feedback
- Adopt the mindset that there is no failure, only feedback
- Feedback is the entire point of a design presentation; treat it as valuable even when you disagree
- Distinguish between an unworkable solution and the valid underlying problem it addresses
- Avoid dismissiveness, defensiveness, or pretending to understand unclear feedback
- Clarify what specific problem the person giving feedback is trying to address

### Seeking Advice
- Practice with colleagues before presenting; get their sense-check on your deck
- Do not present blind — do a full practice run
- Delegate and work as a team; do not try to manage all aspects of a presentation alone

### Using Your Own Voice
- Be genuine; express your actual opinion and show your passion
- Explain your design process and journey — how and why you reached conclusions
- Avoid manufactured overconfidence; it undermines credibility
- Do not say what you think people want to hear

### Respecting the Audience
- Always bring an agenda; state the order of topics and when you want feedback
- If you have met previously, recap what happened before diving into new material
- Integrate previous feedback explicitly to show the audience they were heard
- Never show raw working files or wing it — it is disrespectful of people's time
- Do not assume the audience understands context you have; anticipate and explain

### Making the Work Relatable
- Speak in the first person from the user's perspective rather than referring to "users" as a distant group
- Elicit an emotional response (excitement, worry, recognition) to build commitment
- Demo prototypes wherever possible to make abstract concepts concrete

### Engaging in Dialogue
- Treat the presentation as a discussion, not a monologue
- Ask the audience questions to keep them engaged and signal you want their input
- Design for the "aha moment" — lead people through the story so their minds stay active

### Explaining Why
- Define each problem in a single sentence
- Get explicit audience agreement on problem statements before proposing solutions
- Avoid subjective reasoning ("I chose this because I like it"); ground decisions in evidence
- Show evidence that the problems being solved actually exist

### Utilizing Storytelling
- Sketch out the narrative arc of the presentation early — beginning, middle, and end
- Use Nancy Duarte's contrast framework: show today's painful reality vs. the better future your design enables
- The audience will remember how the story made them feel more than the specific slides

### Keeping It Simple
- "The main thing is to keep the main thing the main thing"
- Strip away everything that is not essential to the mission or message
- Do not overload the audience with too much information
- Summarize at the end: restate objectives, key decisions, and next actions

## Takeaways
- A presentation is a mechanism to reach agreement and drive action — without good presentation skills, good design ideas die on the hard drive.
- Feedback is not an attack; it is the entire purpose of a design review. Never dismiss or deflect it.
- The most resonant presentations follow a clear story arc that contrasts today's pain with a better future — audiences remember feelings, not slides.
- Radical simplicity in structure and content is the hardest and most important discipline in presenting complex design work.
